The $127 Billion Illusion

In September 2013, India's rupee was in freefall. It had lost over 20% of its value against the dollar in a matter of months, driven by the taper tantrum that followed the US Federal Reserve's signal that it would begin withdrawing stimulus. The current account deficit was running at 4.8% of GDP — a level that, in India's history, had previously preceded the 1991 crisis. Foreign exchange reserves were adequate but not comfortable.

The newly appointed Reserve Bank governor, Raghuram Rajan, needed hard currency. Fast.

His solution was an FCNR(B) deposit scheme: India would offer Non-Resident Indians a specially priced three-year foreign currency deposit with the RBI absorbing the currency swap risk. The scheme ran for three months. It raised $34 billion.

That number is routinely cited as evidence of NRI confidence in India. It is cited as evidence of nothing of the sort. It is evidence of what happens when a government offers a sufficiently attractive interest rate to diaspora investors who are already holding dollar savings and are offered a hedged, government-guaranteed instrument with above-market returns.

The $34 billion was not confidence. It was yield-seeking. And the $127 billion sitting on India's books today in accumulated NRI hard-currency deposits is built on the same foundation.

What FCNR(B) actually is

FCNR stands for Foreign Currency Non-Resident (Banks). It is a deposit account held by Non-Resident Indians in India, denominated in a foreign currency — primarily US dollars, though sterling, euro, yen, and Canadian dollars are also permitted. The depositor bears no currency risk: they deposit in dollars and withdraw in dollars. The Indian bank (and through the swap market, ultimately the RBI) absorbs the rupee-dollar exchange risk.

This is categorically different from a foreign direct investment. An FDI investor buys an equity stake or builds productive capacity in India. They take rupee risk. They take business risk. If India's economy deteriorates, they lose. Their money is committed, illiquid, and structurally long on India's economic future.

An FCNR(B) depositor takes none of those risks. They hold a time deposit, denominated in their home currency, at a rate premium over what they could earn abroad, with a government-guaranteed institution backing it. It is a savings account with a patriotism story attached.

"Calling FCNR deposits evidence of NRI confidence in India is like calling a high-yield savings account evidence of confidence in the banking system. The confidence is in the rate, not the story."

The $127 billion in context

India's total NRI deposit book — combining FCNR(B) accounts and NRE (Non-Resident External) rupee term deposits — stood at approximately $127 billion as of the RBI's 2024 data. This figure has grown substantially over the last decade, particularly through periods of rupee weakness when the RBI has signalled it would protect NRI deposit holders from currency losses.

Over the same period, India's net FDI equity inflows tell a different story.

On these numbers, net FDI equity remains larger over the four-year aggregate. But the trajectory is what matters. Net FDI equity has dropped by 40% from FY21 to FY24. Meanwhile, fresh NRI deposit mobilisation has doubled. The composition of India's external financing is shifting — from productive long-term investment toward yield-seeking short-duration deposits. This is the wrong direction.

The crisis test

The best evidence of what NRI deposits really are comes from the moments when the money moved.

In the 2013 taper tantrum, NRI deposit balances fell as depositors — having put money in during calmer times — chose not to renew maturing deposits when uncertainty increased. The RBI had to run the special FCNR(B) scheme precisely because organic deposit renewal was insufficient to maintain the external account balance. The government had to price a crisis instrument to replace money that was walking out.

In 2022, when the rupee again depreciated sharply in response to US rate rises, NRI deposit growth slowed significantly. Fresh inflows barely covered maturities. The RBI responded by raising the interest rate ceiling on NRI deposits in July 2022 — a market intervention to defend the external account that would be unnecessary if the deposits represented unconditional confidence.

The pattern is unmistakeable. Each episode of external account stress has produced a pricing intervention to hold NRI money in place. This is not the behaviour of a country that has secured stable long-term capital. It is the behaviour of a country that has substituted a price lever for a structural one.

What FDI actually measures

When a foreign company establishes a manufacturing unit in India, they are making a bet on India's labour market, its regulatory environment, its infrastructure, its logistics, its judiciary, and the stability of its economic policy. They are taking rupee risk for potentially a decade or more. They cannot exit quickly. Their investment decision reflects a genuine conviction about India's economic trajectory.

When net FDI equity falls from $44 billion to $26 billion in three years, that is 14,000 such decisions, in aggregate, trending toward caution. The reasons are visible in the data: land acquisition difficulties, regulatory uncertainty, infrastructure gaps that remain despite the capital expenditure announcements, and the relentless complexity of operating in India's compliance environment.

No amount of NRI deposit mobilisation repairs those signals. The deposits tell global capital markets that India can attract diaspora savings at the right price. They say nothing about whether India is an attractive place to build something.

"The distinction between NRI deposits and FDI is the distinction between lending and investing. India needs investors, not lenders. The lenders are easier to attract and leave faster."

The illusion in the headline number

$127 billion in NRI deposits is a large and impressive-sounding number. It is used in official communications as evidence of diaspora confidence, of the strength of India's external accounts, of the resilience of its financial system.

But consider what $127 billion in deposits represents relative to $127 billion in FDI equity:

  • The deposits mature and must be repriced or they leave. The FDI equity is permanent.
  • The deposits generate no employment, no technology transfer, no supply chain development. FDI can generate all three.
  • The deposits require periodic incentive interventions to maintain. FDI, once established, tends to generate further FDI as companies expand and create supplier ecosystems.
  • The deposits count toward India's foreign exchange reserves when held at the RBI. When the rupee weakens sharply, these deposits become a liability that must be defended.

The NRI deposit book is not worthless. It provides liquidity, it supports the external account in the short term, and it is a genuine expression of diaspora attachment to India's economy. But it is not a substitute for the productive capital formation that FDI represents, and conflating the two is a category error that has real consequences for policy.

What the data is actually saying

India's trajectory on external financing deserves honest reading. Gross FDI inflows peaked in FY22 and have declined in both subsequent years. Net FDI equity — which accounts for Indian companies investing abroad and profits being repatriated — has fallen even faster, dropping to its lowest level in a decade in FY24.

Meanwhile, India's current account deficit remains structurally negative. It is being financed increasingly by portfolio flows (volatile) and NRI deposits (yield-sensitive). The mix has become more fragile even as the aggregate numbers have grown.

The 2013 episode should be remembered not as a triumph but as a diagnostic. It revealed, in real time, that India's external account could be destabilised quickly and that the stabilisation tool was an above-market rate offer to diaspora savers. Twelve years later, the structural vulnerability is larger. The deposit book is bigger. The net FDI trend is weaker. The repricing interventions have become more frequent.

That is not confidence. That is a recurring confession that the underlying investment story has work to do.
