Both numbers are real. They are not in conflict. They are describing two different countries that happen to share a border and a currency.

One country — let us call it Formal India — grew its economy at 7.8% last fiscal year. Its stock markets are at record highs. Corporate profit margins are at multi-decade peaks. Its IT services sector exported $245 billion worth of software and services. Its financial sector is profitable and well-capitalised. Its construction and infrastructure pipeline has never been larger.

The other country — Informal India — employs 93% of the workforce. Its casual workers earned roughly the same in real terms last year as they did five years ago. Agricultural wage growth, adjusted for rural inflation, has been flat to negative across most of the period. The household savings rate has declined. Consumer debt has risen. The informal sector is working harder to stand still.

The question is not whether India is growing. It clearly is. The question is: who is growing, and who is being counted when the headline number is published?

What GDP actually measures

India's GDP methodology follows the expenditure approach — it aggregates consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports. The number is real and methodologically sound. But GDP has a structural bias: it captures formal economic activity far more accurately than informal activity.

When a large IT firm earns a margin, that's in the accounts. When a street vendor earns ₹400 a day, that's estimated and imputed — and systematically undercounted when economic stress compresses informal margins. During growth periods, formal sector productivity can surge without a proportional informal-sector response. GDP captures the surge. The survey data captures the flatness.

This is not a data conspiracy. It is a measurement limitation that is well-understood by economists and systematically ignored by commentators.

"GDP at 7.8% and wages at zero are not contradictions. They are a precise description of who owns the productivity gains."

What the wages are actually doing

The Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS), published annually by MoSPI, is the most reliable source of wage data in India. The Annual Reports for FY19 through FY24 tell a consistent story.

For casual wage workers — the largest category, comprising daily labourers, agricultural workers, and unorganised construction workers — nominal daily wages have risen from approximately ₹315 per day in FY19 to approximately ₹375 per day in FY24. That is an 19% nominal increase over five years.

Against a Consumer Price Index (CPI) rise of roughly 28% over the same period, that nominal increase is a real-terms decline. The casual worker in FY24 earns meaningfully less in purchasing power than they earned in FY19 — the year before COVID, before supply shocks, before the inflation cycle that eroded whatever nominal gains accrued.

Regular salaried workers fared better but not dramatically so. Real wage growth for this group has been positive but thin — averaging perhaps 1.5–2% per year. The gains have been concentrated at the upper end of the distribution. A software engineer at a listed technology company has seen real compensation grow substantially. A mid-level data entry worker at an unlisted SME has seen something closer to stagnation.

The pattern is consistent across six years: GDP grows, wages nominally tick up, inflation erases the gain, real purchasing power is flat to negative for the majority of the workforce.

The structural divide

India's economic growth story of the last decade has been driven by sectors with two characteristics: high productivity and low direct employment. Information technology, financial services, pharmaceuticals, and infrastructure construction all generate large value-added figures relative to the number of workers they directly employ.

Meanwhile, agriculture — which still employs approximately 44% of India's workforce — has grown at under 4% per year in most recent years. Manufacturing, which is where most economies have historically anchored mass employment and wage growth, has remained stuck at approximately 15–17% of GDP since 2012. The manufacturing employment share has barely moved.

The asymmetry is visible immediately. The sector employing nearly half of India's workers produces 17% of GDP and grows at 3.5%. The formal services sector employing 27% of workers produces 53% of GDP and grows at 8.4%. These are two different economic realities running in parallel, averaged into a single headline number.

Corporate profits and labour's share

A useful cross-check is labour's share of GDP — the proportion of national income that flows to workers as wages versus the proportion that flows to capital as profits.

Data from CMIE's corporate database and RBI's National Accounts shows that India's labour share of GDP has been in gradual decline since the mid-2000s. Corporate profit margins for listed companies reached their highest levels in over a decade in FY23 and FY24. Net profit as a share of net sales for the Nifty 500 touched multi-year highs.

These facts are not incompatible with GDP growth. In fact, they explain it. An economy can grow rapidly when capital is being allocated efficiently and corporates are extracting value. The question is whether that growth distributes broadly or concentrates narrowly. The PLFS and corporate data, read together, answer that question: it is concentrating.

"The informal sector is not failing to grow. It is growing — just not fast enough to outrun inflation, and its workers are paying the gap out of their purchasing power."

The K-shape, explained plainly

After COVID, economists began using the term "K-shaped recovery" to describe a divergence where some parts of the economy recovered and continued upward while other parts recovered only partially before flattening. India is a textbook case — except the K-shape predates COVID. The pandemic accelerated and sharpened a divergence that had been widening since at least 2016.

The upper arm of the K: listed companies, formal salaried workers, property owners, financial asset holders. These groups have seen genuine wealth accumulation over the decade. Stock market indices are at all-time highs. Premium real estate has appreciated substantially. The top income decile has captured a disproportionate share of growth.

The lower arm: casual workers, agricultural labourers, small traders, self-employed artisans. This is the majority of India. This group has seen nominal income growth that fails to keep pace with the cost of food, fuel, and education. The household savings rate for this group has declined. The consumer credit they are taking on to cover the gap shows up as a risk in the banking system.

Why both numbers can be true at the same time

There is no contradiction in India's headline GDP figure and the wage data. They are measuring different things.

GDP measures the total value of goods and services produced. It can grow when a small number of high-productivity sectors expand, even if the majority of the workforce is stationary or declining in real terms. A country of 1.4 billion people can produce a large and growing economic output while the median worker experiences stagnation. The US demonstrated this clearly between 1990 and 2020. India is demonstrating it now, at a larger scale and a faster pace.

The 7.8% is not a lie. It is an accurate description of formal economic output. The 0% wage growth is not a grievance narrative. It is an accurate description of what the PLFS shows when you adjust for inflation.

They coexist because India has two economies — one formal, capital-intensive, globally competitive, and growing rapidly; one informal, labour-intensive, domestically-dependent, and treading water. As long as growth is driven primarily by the first, the second will continue to be averaged in without being transformed.

What would actually change this

The economic literature is relatively clear on what converts productivity growth into broad wage growth: labour-intensive manufacturing at scale, agricultural productivity improvements that release workers into higher-wage sectors, and a robust, competitive services sector accessible to workers without degrees.

India has made progress on the third. The first remains elusive — PLI schemes have had modest success but have not produced the manufacturing employment surge that comparable programmes produced in South Korea or China in earlier decades. Agricultural productivity remains constrained by land fragmentation, water stress, and MSP policy that incentivises the wrong crops.

None of this is unsolvable. India has the capital markets, the technical capacity, and the demographic dividend to engineer a different growth trajectory. The question is whether the headline number — that comfortable 7.8% — creates enough political pressure to demand change, or enough complacency to avoid it.

So far, the evidence suggests the latter.
